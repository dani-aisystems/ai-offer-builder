# Offer principles

How to think about the offer before any slide exists. Read this before Phase 3 (packages) and again when you plan the deck in Phase 4. It holds what makes an offer strong for this client; `deck-design-and-copy.md` holds how it looks and reads, and `workflow-and-qa.md` holds how a run is checked.

## The nine layers

A deck moves through these layers in order. Each layer answers one question and owns one slide or group of slides. A fact or recommendation belongs to exactly one layer and is not restated in the next.

| Layer | Question it answers | Typical slide |
|---|---|---|
| Research | What do we actually know about this business? | none (it feeds the others) |
| Diagnosis | What is strong, weak, missing or underused today? | Analysis |
| Opportunity | Why does fixing it matter commercially, and why now? | Why this matters |
| Strategy | What should the site do, for whom, in what order? | Strategy |
| Scope | What exactly will be built? | Package slides |
| Packages | What are the three levels of investment? | Package slides, comparison |
| Recommendation | Which configuration fits this business, and why? | Personalised offer |
| ROI | How could the investment pay for itself, on stated assumptions? | Expected return |
| Next steps | What must the client do to start? | Next steps |

Consecutive strategic slides must answer different questions. Opportunity explains why change matters (business impact, customer journey, trust). Diagnosis states what is happening today, with concrete findings and real strengths. Strategy defines what to build and why each part exists. If two of these slides could swap their bullets without anyone noticing, one of them is wrong: rewrite it from its own question.

## Research becomes a normalised brief first

Before writing copy, turn everything known about the client (research, call notes, supplied documents, site and listing checks) into one brief with three labelled groups:

- **Verified**: confirmed in a source you opened, or supplied by the user, with where it came from.
- **Assumption or estimate**: anything the user estimated, any hypothetical, any default you chose.
- **Open**: needed but unknown; ask or leave it out.

Only the verified group and labelled assumptions may reach a slide. When a finding comes from a third-party listing rather than the client, the slide says what "public listings show" and the record marks it "to confirm with the owner". When the user says "confirm it yourself" or "you determine", do the check or choose a default, say which, and show your rationale; do not hand the question back.

## Personalisation is structural, not cosmetic

Before drafting, write down for this client:

- brand identity and visual language
- positioning, cuisine or service concept, price level
- location and who comes (locals, tourists, groups, B2B)
- how customers reach them and book today
- current site or listing condition: strengths and weaknesses
- relevant reviews or reputation facts that were supplied or verified
- the owner's likely priorities and the key conversion actions

Every major recommendation traces back to one of these lines. A deck where only the name, logo and colours change while the strategy stays generic fails this test. Test it: swap in another client's name. If the slide still reads true, it is not personalised.

Design personalisation comes from the client's own visuals (logo, photos, existing site, signage) and follows `deck-design-and-copy.md`.

## Packages

Three packages that are really one product with a price step are a failure. Differentiate on dimensions that matter to this business, and let the progression feel natural rather than withholding trivial features:

- scope: page count and which pages (for example a groups page, an about page)
- strategic depth: how much of the strategy slide each tier implements
- content: languages, content support, gallery and review sections
- catalogue or menu implementation
- booking and conversion: enquiry form, reservation flow, call and map actions
- SEO depth: basics, local, structured data
- integrations and analytics: tracking, reports
- ongoing support: scope and fee
- revisions and advanced features

Rules:

- **Every feature must be something the site build delivers.** A feature that only exists in another service (running ads, managing a social account) is not a package feature. If the agency also sells such a service, show it as a separate optional add-on line. Include a service in a tier only when the agency has confirmed it delivers it and wants it there.
- **Each tier includes the one below it.** Say so once ("includes everything in Package 1"), so the progression is obvious and the lists stay short.
- **The step up has a reason the owner can repeat.** If you cannot state in one sentence what Package 2 adds that this client needs, rework the tier.
- **Keep package names** the agency uses (translated naturally, or the agency's own). Do not rename tiers per client.
- **Offer the client a choice where a decision is genuinely theirs**, for example their own reservation system inside the site or an outside provider. Note in the record what the choice costs and what is billed separately.

## Pricing

Derive prices from scope, complexity, the client's business context, the agency's own rates and the value delivered. Use ratios between tiers as a sanity check, not as the method. Never reuse another client's numbers. When a tier's content changes (for example a service is removed), re-derive its price from what is left and explain the change to the user. Keep all prices, support fees and the recommended tier's price identical wherever they repeat (package slide, comparison, recommendation, next steps, record).

Show prices so the decision is easy: one price and one monthly fee per package, the recommended package visually first, and anything optional clearly outside the price. State payment terms, deposit share and timing only as the user supplied them.

## Recommendation

The recommended package is the one that matches the business situation, and the slide says why in terms of the client's own findings: business situation, then website need, then selected scope. "Best value" alone is not a reason. Name what the owner gets from that tier that the tier below lacks, and what they can add later.

## Trust before price

A client who has not met the agency is asked for money on slide 5. Give them a reason to trust the sender before the first package appears: a short "why us" slide after strategy and before packages.

- Build it around what business owners value when choosing a web designer: results for the business, trust and proof, clear communication, reliability and ongoing support. Research this (Phase 2) rather than writing about the agency's own favourite strengths.
- Use proof that is real and usable: a project the agency has delivered for a comparable business, figures the user supplied, who the owner will talk to, fixed price and clear scope. One proof point that matches the client's sector beats several generic ones.
- Label estimates as estimates. If a number has not been measured, say "by our estimate".
- Do not cite a project or a person without the user's confirmation that the agency may name them.

## ROI

Keep the business case short enough to check in the owner's head. Every number is one of the four figure types in SKILL.md. Show the inputs, the arithmetic and the period: "if the site brings N extra bookings a month at an average of X, that is Y a month, so the package is recovered in about Z months". State whether the figure is revenue or profit and keep it the same throughout. Never present an industry benchmark as this client's result.

## Next steps

A short, low-friction sequence the client can follow without questions: choose a package, confirm scope, deposit (only as supplied), send materials, build and review, launch. Include one clear call to action and the agency's contact details from Phase 0.
